"Won't bleach make my teeth weak?" Somebody's aunt has usually told them it will. It's a fair worry, and the short answer is reassuring: whitening done properly, under a dentist's eye, doesn't damage healthy enamel. The longer answer is worth knowing, because there are a few situations where care really is needed.

What the research says

Professional whitening has been used and studied for more than thirty years. When the gels are used at the strengths and times they're designed for, studies have generally found no meaningful harm to the enamel or to the nerve inside the tooth. Enamel may lose a tiny amount of mineral on the surface straight after treatment, but saliva puts it back over the following days. That's one reason we suggest fluoride toothpaste during and after a whitening course.

Problems tend to come from misuse: gels that are far too strong, used too often, for too long, or without anyone checking the mouth first. Cheap products bought online, especially those with unclear ingredients, are where most of the horror stories come from.

Sensitivity, the side effect you might notice

The common side effect is sensitivity. Some people feel short, sharp twinges to cold air or cold drinks during treatment or for a day or two after. A few feel nothing at all. It happens because the peroxide passes through the enamel and briefly irritates the nerve. It's temporary and goes away on its own once whitening stops.

If you already have sensitive teeth, tell us. We can start you on a sensitivity toothpaste a couple of weeks beforehand, use a gel with desensitising agents in it, shorten the wear time for home trays or space out the sessions. With home whitening you can simply skip a day if your teeth feel tingly. Avoid very cold drinks for the first day or two after a clinic session.

Gums

Strong gel on the gums can cause a white patch and some soreness. That's why in-clinic whitening always uses a barrier to cover the gums, and why home trays are made to fit your own teeth closely. If any gel does touch the gum, the irritation usually clears within a day.

When we'd advise against it, or say wait

Whitening isn't for everyone, at least not straight away. We'd hold off if you have untreated decay, leaking fillings, cracked teeth or gums that are actively inflamed, because peroxide can hurt in all of those. Exposed roots from receding gums are also sensitive and won't whiten the way enamel does.

We don't whiten teeth during pregnancy or while breastfeeding, simply as a precaution. And for teenagers whose teeth are still maturing, we usually suggest waiting.

If you have crowns, veneers or tooth-coloured fillings on your front teeth, remember they won't lighten. You might end up with natural teeth that are whiter than your old crown, so plan for replacing it to match afterwards.

A few myths

Whitening doesn't thin your teeth or make them more likely to break. It doesn't strip off a layer of enamel the way a sander would. It also won't make teeth look see-through when done properly. If anything, teeth that look slightly translucent straight after treatment settle back within a day or two.

Lemon juice, baking soda scrubs and charcoal, on the other hand, are home remedies we'd steer you away from. Acid and harsh abrasives can do real damage to enamel, and once enamel is worn away it doesn't grow back.
