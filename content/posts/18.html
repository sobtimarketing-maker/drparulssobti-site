Open wide in front of a mirror and you may spot them at the back: dark grey fillings, put in perhaps twenty or thirty years ago. A lot of people in their forties and fifties have a few. We're often asked whether they should come out. Sometimes yes, often not yet, and the reasons are worth understanding before you decide.

What silver fillings are

The grey fillings are dental amalgam, a mix of silver, tin, copper and mercury. For well over a century it was the standard material for back teeth because it's hard-wearing and cheap. Plenty of amalgam fillings have lasted decades.

The mercury worries people, understandably. Once it's mixed into the filling it's bound up in the alloy, and major health bodies have looked at the evidence repeatedly and consider amalgam safe for most people. Many countries are now phasing it out, mainly because of the mercury it puts into the environment when it's made and thrown away. So we don't recommend drilling out a sound amalgam filling just because it contains mercury. Removing one actually releases more vapour than leaving it alone, which is why, when we do replace them, we use rubber dam isolation and strong suction.

When replacement makes sense

The best reason to replace an old filling is that it's failing. Over the years the edges can break down and leave a tiny gap where bacteria get in, and decay can start underneath without any pain. We look for this at check-ups and on X-rays. A filling that has cracked, or a tooth with a crack running out from a large filling, is another clear reason, because amalgam doesn't support the walls of the tooth and big ones can let a cusp snap off.

Then there's appearance. Amalgam can turn the surrounding tooth a dull grey over time, and a dark filling in a premolar can show when you laugh. If those teeth are in your smile line and they bother you, wanting them tooth-coloured is a perfectly good reason, as long as you understand what's involved.

What replaces them

For small and medium fillings we usually use composite, the same tooth-coloured material we talked about in the bonding post. It's bonded to the tooth, so we can often remove less healthy structure than an amalgam needed. It does take longer to place, needs the tooth kept completely dry, and on very large fillings it can wear faster than amalgam.

Where a big old filling has left thin, weak walls, a direct filling may not be enough. An inlay or onlay, made in ceramic to fit the tooth exactly and then bonded in, holds the tooth together better. In the most broken-down teeth a crown is the safer choice. We'll tell you which one the tooth needs once the old filling is out, because sometimes there's more decay underneath than an X-ray shows.

Doing it sensibly

If you have several old fillings, we rarely replace them all in one go unless they all need it. We'd start with any that are leaking or cracked, then deal with the ones that show when you smile, and leave sound ones at the back alone until they need attention. Spreading the work out also spreads the cost, and you can see the price ranges for crowns on our dental crown cost page.

A tooth can feel a bit sensitive to cold for a week or two after a filling is changed. That usually settles. If it doesn't, let us know.
