Look straight at a pleasing smile and the front teeth seem to step back in a steady rhythm. For about fifty years dentists have described that rhythm with a number borrowed from art and architecture: the golden proportion.

What the golden proportion is

The golden proportion is a ratio of roughly 1.618 to 1. It turns up in the spiral of a shell, the layout of classical buildings and the proportions artists have used for centuries. In 1973 a dentist named Lombardi suggested that the upper front teeth in an attractive smile follow it too, and in 1978 another dentist, Levin, turned the idea into a measuring grid.

The idea works on how wide each tooth looks from the front, not how wide it really is. Because the arch curves away, each tooth appears narrower than the one in front of it. In a golden smile the visible widths are:

  • Central incisor: 1.618
  • Lateral incisor: 1
  • Canine: 0.618

So each tooth appears about 62 per cent as wide as the tooth in front of it. The photograph on our home page is marked up this way.

Why it is useful

The ratio gives a starting point when teeth are being rebuilt from nothing: after an accident, with heavily worn teeth, or when a full set of veneers or crowns is being made. It helps a dentist and technician agree on widths before a single tooth is shaped, and it explains why a smile with a too-wide lateral incisor, or canines that look as broad as the centrals, can feel crowded or flat.

When to ignore it

Here is the honest part. When researchers measured large groups of natural smiles that people found attractive, very few followed the golden proportion exactly. Many attractive smiles have laterals that look a little wider than the formula allows, and a strict golden ratio can make the side teeth look too narrow on a broad face.

So dentists treat it as one guide among several:

  • Width to length. A central incisor usually looks best when its width is about 75 to 80 per cent of its length. Much squarer and it looks short; much longer and it looks narrow.
  • Even stepping. Some dentists prefer a gentler, constant step from tooth to tooth, sometimes called the recurring esthetic dental proportion, which suits longer teeth.
  • The face. The width of the smile should suit the width of the face, and the centre of the smile should sit on the facial midline.
  • The person. Some people want a soft, youthful look, others a bolder one. Their own teeth, if they like them, are often the best guide of all.
A ratio can tell you where to start. Only the face in front of you can tell you where to stop.

How we use it

In a smile design, the proportions are drawn over photographs of the patient's own face. If the golden ratio suits that face, we use it. If it makes the smile look narrow or artificial, we adjust it. The patient then sees the result on screen, and often as a trial smile in their own mouth, before anything is made.
