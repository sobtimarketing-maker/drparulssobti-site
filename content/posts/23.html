"Will you have to file my teeth down?" It's the question that stops a lot of people from even asking about veneers. They've heard stories, or seen photos of teeth ground to little stumps. For many cases the answer is that very little needs to be removed, and for some, nothing at all.

Why teeth are usually prepared

A porcelain veneer has thickness, usually somewhere around half a millimetre, sometimes less. If it's bonded onto an untouched tooth, that tooth ends up slightly fuller at the front. Sometimes that's fine. Often it isn't, because the tooth would look bulky and the edge near the gum can trap plaque.

So in a standard veneer, a thin layer of enamel is removed to make room. This is very different from the heavy reduction for a full crown. Done properly, the preparation stays within the enamel, which matters because veneers bond far more strongly to enamel than to the dentin underneath.

What no-prep means

A no-prep veneer is bonded to the tooth without any shaping of the enamel, apart from perhaps a light polish of the surface. They're made very thin, often as fine as a contact lens, which is only possible with certain strong ceramics and careful lab work.

The appeal is obvious. There's usually no need for an injection, and in principle the tooth underneath stays as it was, so the treatment can be reversed more easily than with prepared veneers.

Minimal-prep: the middle ground

Minimal-prep veneers sit between the two. We remove only small amounts of enamel in the places that need it, for instance smoothing a corner that sticks out or making a little room at the gum edge so the veneer finishes neatly. Many cases that people think of as "no-prep" are really this, and it often gives a more natural result than forcing a veneer onto a tooth that wasn't quite ready for it.

Who can have them

No-prep and minimal-prep veneers work best when the plan is to add to the teeth rather than take away. That usually means teeth that are small, short, worn or a little behind their neighbours, or smiles with gaps to close. Narrow teeth in a wide smile, where dark spaces show at the corners when you smile, can also be good candidates.

They don't suit teeth that are already large or pushed forward, because adding thickness would only make them stand out more. They're also a poor fit for very dark teeth. A thin ceramic shell can't fully hide a strong grey or brown colour underneath, so a little more thickness, and therefore some preparation, is needed to mask it.

How we decide

Digital planning makes this much less of a guess. We design the new smile on screen first, then try it in your mouth as a temporary mock-up. Because the mock-up shows exactly where the final veneers will sit, we can see where the new outline is already outside your existing tooth and where it isn't. In many cases that means preparing only through the mock-up itself, removing enamel only where the design needs space.
