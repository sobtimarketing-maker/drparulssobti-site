Ask most people what colour their teeth should be and they will say white. Ask a dentist and they will ask which white. Natural teeth are not one colour at all, and understanding why is the key to a smile that looks real.

Why teeth are not simply white

A tooth is built in layers. The inner layer, dentine, is naturally yellowish. Over it sits enamel, which is partly see-through. The colour you see is the dentine glowing through the enamel, so a tooth is never one flat shade. It is usually a little warmer near the gum, where the enamel is thin, and lighter and more translucent towards the biting edge.

The three parts of a shade

Dentists describe tooth colour in three ways, borrowed from colour science:

  • Hue. The basic colour family: more yellow-brown, more reddish, more grey. Most natural teeth sit in a narrow yellow to orange range.
  • Chroma. How strong or saturated that colour is. A tooth with high chroma looks deeper and more yellow; low chroma looks paler.
  • Value. How light or dark the tooth is, from bright to greyish. Of the three, value matters most. A tooth with the wrong brightness stands out from across a room, even when the hue is right.

Translucency and the small details

Real teeth also have translucency, especially along the edges, where light passes through the enamel and can look faintly blue or grey. Many have small white flecks, subtle bands or a soft glow called opalescence. These details are what make a tooth look alive. A crown or veneer that is a perfect opaque white can look more like a bathroom tile than a tooth.

How shade is measured

The most familiar tool is a shade guide: a set of sample teeth labelled with codes such as A1, A2, B1 and so on. The letter is the hue family and the number is the depth, with lower numbers lighter. Many clinics also use guides arranged by value, since brightness is what the eye notices first.

Shade-taking is done carefully because colour changes with light:

  • at the start of the visit, before the teeth dry out, since dry enamel looks whiter than it really is;
  • in good, neutral light, ideally close to daylight;
  • in photographs taken with the shade sample beside the teeth, so the ceramist sees exactly what the dentist saw.
The right shade is not the whitest one. It is the one that looks like it grew there.

Choosing a shade that suits you

A good shade suits the whites of your eyes, your skin tone and your age. Very bright, very opaque teeth can look striking in a photograph and artificial in conversation. When people want a brighter smile, the usual plan is to whiten the natural teeth first and then match any crowns or veneers to the new shade, because porcelain does not whiten later.
