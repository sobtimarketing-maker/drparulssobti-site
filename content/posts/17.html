Chalky white patches on the front teeth are surprisingly common. Some people have had them since childhood. Others notice them for the first time when braces come off. They aren't usually harmful, but they can be hard to ignore once you've seen them in a selfie. Most of them can be made far less visible while leaving the healthy tooth around them alone.

Where white spots come from

A white spot is a patch where the enamel is less mineralised than the rest. It scatters light differently, so it looks opaque and paler than the enamel next to it. The reason it formed matters, because it decides what will work.

Fluorosis is one cause. When a child takes in more fluoride than they need while the adult teeth are still forming under the gums, the enamel can develop with faint white streaks or flecks. In parts of India the groundwater is naturally high in fluoride, so we see this quite often in people who grew up in those areas. Severe fluorosis can also bring brown staining or small pits, but the mild kind is just white mottling.

Another common cause is early decay. Plaque left around brackets during orthodontic treatment, or along the gum line on any tooth, slowly draws minerals out of the enamel and leaves a white patch. These are often in a ring shape where a bracket used to sit.

Sometimes a single tooth has a white or yellowish patch because a baby tooth above it had an infection or knock years ago, which disturbed the adult tooth as it formed. And some people simply have patchy enamel from birth.

First, make sure the spot isn't active

A white spot from early decay can still be getting worse. Before any cosmetic work we check whether the surface is soft or hard and whether there's plaque sitting on it. If it's active, the first job is to stop it, with better cleaning in that area, fluoride toothpaste and sometimes a fluoride varnish or remineralising paste. Many fresh spots after braces fade noticeably over a few months with this alone, so it's worth waiting before doing anything more.

Micro-abrasion

For spots that sit in the outer surface of the enamel, micro-abrasion can work well. We apply a mild acid paste with a fine abrasive and polish it over the patch, removing a very thin layer of the affected enamel. It doesn't need an injection and is usually done in a single visit. The tooth often looks a little more yellow afterwards because the surface is thinner, which is why we sometimes pair it with whitening.

Resin infiltration

Resin infiltration is a newer technique and we like it for many of these cases because almost nothing is drilled away. The spot is gently etched to open up its porous surface, dried, and then a very runny resin is soaked into it and set with a light. Once the pores are filled, the patch reflects light much more like normal enamel, so it blends in. It works best on spots from early decay and on milder fluorosis. Deeper or older patches may only partly fade.

Bonding and veneers

If a spot is deep or the other methods don't take it far enough, a thin layer of composite bonding can cover it, usually after removing just the surface of the patch so the material can sit flush. When many teeth are affected, or there's brown staining and pitting as well, porcelain veneers may give a more even result, though we'd only suggest them after trying the gentler options.

A word about whitening

People often ask whether whitening will hide white spots. It can go either way. Straight after whitening the spots can look more obvious for a few days, then as the rest of the tooth lightens the contrast often softens. It rarely removes them on its own, but it can help as part of a plan with micro-abrasion or infiltration.

We can't always tell from a photo which approach will work, so at your visit we'll look at the spots under good light, check how deep they seem and talk through the options in the order we'd try them.
