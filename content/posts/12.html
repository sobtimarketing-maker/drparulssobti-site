Whitening is the treatment people ask us about most. The usual first question is whether to do it in the clinic or at home. Both work. They suit different people, and quite often the best results come from using a bit of both.

How whitening works at all

Every professional whitening product uses the same basic ingredient: a peroxide gel, either hydrogen peroxide or carbamide peroxide, which breaks down into hydrogen peroxide in the mouth. The peroxide soaks into the enamel and breaks up the coloured molecules that have built up inside it over the years. So the change happens inside the tooth itself.

That's also why whitening only works on natural teeth. Crowns, veneers and tooth-coloured fillings won't change shade, which matters if you have any of them on your front teeth.

Before either kind of whitening we clean the teeth and check for decay, leaking fillings and exposed roots, since peroxide on any of those can hurt.

Whitening in the clinic

In-clinic whitening uses a stronger gel, so it has to be done by us with the gums protected. We put a barrier over the gums, apply the gel to the front teeth, leave it for a set time and then refresh it, usually a few rounds in the same sitting. Some systems use a light alongside the gel.

The main advantage is speed. You walk out with visibly lighter teeth after one visit, which is why people often choose it before a wedding or a big family function. The drawback is that a fast change can bring more sensitivity for a day or two, and some of the immediate brightness settles back a little over the following days as the teeth rehydrate.

Whitening at home

Take-home whitening uses custom trays made from a scan of your teeth, so they fit closely and keep the gel on the teeth and off the gums. You put a small amount of a milder gel in the trays and wear them for the time we advise, usually daily for around two weeks.

It's slower, but you can control it. If your teeth get sensitive, you skip a night. If you reach a shade you like before the course is over, you stop. The change tends to be steady and holds well. And you keep the trays, so a short top-up later only needs a fresh tube of gel.

The catch is that it depends on you wearing the trays as advised. Missed nights simply mean a slower result.

What about strips and whitening toothpaste?

Over-the-counter strips contain much weaker peroxide and one-size-fits-all strips that don't reach every surface, so results tend to be patchy and modest. Whitening toothpastes mostly polish off surface stain with mild abrasives. They can keep a clean smile looking fresh, but they won't change the shade of the tooth itself. Charcoal powders are best avoided, because they can be quite abrasive on enamel.

What to expect

Yellowish teeth usually respond best. Grey or banded staining, like the tetracycline stains we covered earlier, responds more slowly and sometimes only partly. Results vary from person to person, and nobody can promise an exact shade in advance.
