When a dentist studies your smile, they use a handful of words that can sound like jargon. Each one names something simple you can see in a mirror. Learn them and you will understand exactly what a smile design is trying to change.

The lip line

The lip line is how high your upper lip rises when you smile fully. Dentists usually describe it in three ways:

  • Low. The lip covers part of the upper teeth and none of the gum shows.
  • Average. The whole length of the front teeth shows, with just the tips of gum between them.
  • High. A band of gum shows above the teeth. Up to two or three millimetres is common and usually looks natural. More than that is often called a gummy smile.

The lip line matters because it decides what needs attention. With a low lip line, the gum line is rarely seen, so the focus is on the teeth. With a high lip line, the shape and level of the gums matter just as much as the teeth.

The smile line

The smile line, also called the smile arc, is the curve formed by the biting edges of the upper front teeth. In most pleasing smiles it runs parallel to the curve of the lower lip, rising gently towards the corners of the mouth.

When the edges form a flat line, or curve the wrong way, the smile can look older or worn. This is common when teeth have been ground down over the years. Restoring a gentle curve is one of the quiet changes that makes a smile look younger without making it look done.

Buccal corridors

Buccal corridors are the small dark spaces at the corners of the mouth, between the back teeth and the inside of the cheeks, that you see when you smile broadly.

A little dark space looks natural and gives the smile depth. Very wide corridors can make a smile look narrow or sunken. Filling them completely can make it look like a row of teeth with no shadow at all, which is just as unnatural. When a smile looks too narrow, the cause is usually the shape or position of the side teeth. Sometimes it can be improved by widening the arch with aligners, or by adding a little bulk to the premolars with veneers or bonding.

Two more words you may hear

  • Incisal display. How much of the upper front teeth shows when your lips are relaxed and slightly apart. A few millimetres is typical in younger adults, and it tends to shrink with age.
  • Midline. The line between the two upper central incisors. It should be close to the centre of the face and vertical, not tilted.
None of these is a rule to be forced onto every face. They are a vocabulary, so patient and dentist can talk about the same thing.

How this is recorded

At a smile design consultation these features are recorded with photographs and short videos of you speaking and smiling naturally, because a posed smile can hide what an everyday smile shows. They are then measured on screen, and the plan is drawn over your own face.
