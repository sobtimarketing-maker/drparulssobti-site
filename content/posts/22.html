Once someone decides veneers are right for them, the next question is nearly always which kind. Composite or porcelain? In the clinic we usually call them direct and indirect, which describes how each one is made. Neither is simply better. They suit different situations, and this post lays out how they compare.

Direct veneers: built on the tooth

A direct veneer is made from composite resin, the tooth-coloured material we described in the bonding post. It's applied straight onto your tooth in layers, shaped by hand, set with a curing light and polished. There's no lab involved, so the whole thing is usually finished in the chair, often in a single long appointment for a set of front teeth.

Very little tooth needs to be removed, and sometimes none at all. That makes direct veneers easy to undo or change later, which is a real advantage for younger patients or anyone unsure how far they want to go.

Indirect veneers: made in the lab

An indirect veneer is made outside the mouth, usually from ceramic. After the design is agreed and tested as a trial smile, we prepare the teeth, scan them and send the design to be made. Our in-house lab handles a good deal of this work. You wear temporaries for a short while, and at the next visit the finished veneers are checked, adjusted and bonded on.

Ceramic veneers need more planning and usually some reshaping of the enamel, though often less than people fear. How much depends on the design, and the next post covers cases where very little or none is needed.

How they look

Both can look natural in skilled hands. Porcelain has an edge here because it handles light much like enamel does, with a slight glow and translucency at the edges, and it keeps that look for years. Composite can look excellent on the day, but it relies heavily on the dentist's layering skill and it gradually loses some of its surface shine.

How they hold up

Porcelain is harder and very resistant to staining. Tea, coffee and paan that would slowly discolour composite have little effect on a glazed ceramic surface. Composite is softer, so edges can chip and the surface can pick up stain over time, which is why direct veneers need occasional polishing. With good care, porcelain veneers generally last considerably longer before they need replacing.

When something goes wrong

Here composite has the advantage. A chip in a direct veneer can usually be repaired in one short visit by adding fresh material. A chipped porcelain veneer can sometimes be polished or patched, but a larger break usually means making a new one.

Cost

Direct veneers cost less per tooth, mostly because there's no lab work and fewer visits. Porcelain costs more upfront. Over many years the gap can narrow, since composite needs more upkeep and replacing sooner, but the first bill is what most people notice. Our starting prices are on the veneers page, and we always give you a written quote per tooth.

So which one?

We often suggest composite for small changes to a few teeth, especially in younger patients whose gums and bite may still change. Porcelain tends to make more sense for bigger changes in colour or shape across several teeth, or when someone wants a result that will look the same for many years. Some smiles use both. At your consultation we'll show you what each would look like on your own teeth before you choose.
